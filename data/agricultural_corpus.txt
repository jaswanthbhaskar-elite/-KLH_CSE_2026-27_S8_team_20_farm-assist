AGRICULTURAL KNOWLEDGE CORPUS
This corpus is a reference text used to demonstrate the Farm Assist string
matching algorithms (KMP, Rabin-Karp, Aho-Corasick) on a realistic body of
text, instead of only on short one-line records from the structured data
files. Each section below covers a different agricultural topic.

SECTION 1: MAJOR CROPS
Rice is a staple cereal crop grown mainly during the Kharif season in
clay and loamy soils that can hold standing water. It requires high
water availability and performs best with transplanted seedlings in
puddled fields. Wheat is a Rabi season crop that prefers loamy soil with
medium water requirement and cool growing temperatures during grain
filling. Maize is a versatile Kharif crop grown in loamy soil with
medium water needs; it responds strongly to nitrogen and is often used
both as a food grain and as fodder. Cotton is a long duration Kharif
crop grown in black soil, valued for its fiber, and needs a warm growing
season with well distributed but moderate rainfall. Groundnut grows well
in sandy soil with low water requirement and is an important oilseed and
nitrogen fixing legume crop. Sugarcane is a long duration crop that needs
heavy irrigation, rich loamy soil, and warm humid conditions to build up
sugar content in the stalk.

SECTION 2: SOIL REQUIREMENTS
Soil type strongly influences which crop will thrive. Clay soil retains
water well and suits paddy rice cultivation but drains slowly, which can
harm crops that need aerated roots. Loamy soil is a balanced mixture of
sand, silt and clay, drains reasonably well while still retaining
moisture and nutrients, and is considered ideal for wheat, maize and
sugarcane. Sandy soil drains quickly and warms up fast in spring, which
suits groundnut and other crops that dislike waterlogging, but it holds
fewer nutrients and needs more frequent fertilizer application. Black
soil, also called regur soil, is rich in clay and retains moisture for
long periods, making it well suited to cotton cultivation in low
rainfall regions. Soil pH also matters: most crops prefer a near neutral
pH between six and seven, while very acidic or very alkaline soil can
lock up nutrients and stunt growth even when fertilizer is applied.

SECTION 3: IRRIGATION METHODS
Flood irrigation, where whole fields are submerged, is traditionally
used for rice because standing water suppresses weeds and stabilizes
soil temperature. Furrow irrigation channels water along small trenches
between crop rows and is common for maize, cotton and sugarcane, using
gravity to move water without wetting the entire field surface. Drip
irrigation delivers water directly to the root zone through emitters and
is highly water efficient, making it valuable for groundnut, cotton and
horticultural crops grown in water scarce sandy soils. Sprinkler
irrigation mimics rainfall over a wide area and suits wheat and other
field crops on gently sloping land where furrows are impractical.
Efficient irrigation scheduling, matched to each crop's growth stage,
reduces water stress during flowering and grain filling, which are the
periods most sensitive to drought.

SECTION 4: NUTRIENTS AND FERTILIZERS
Nitrogen is the nutrient most responsible for vegetative growth and leaf
greenness; nitrogen deficiency shows up as yellowing of older leaves and
stunted growth, and is commonly corrected with Urea. Phosphorus supports
strong root development, flowering and seed formation; a phosphorus
deficiency often appears as dark purplish leaves and delayed maturity,
and is treated with DAP or SSP. Potassium improves overall plant vigor,
disease resistance and water regulation within the plant; potassium
deficiency shows as scorched or browning leaf edges and weak stems, and
is corrected with MOP. Sulphur deficiency causes pale yellow young
leaves and reduced oil content in oilseed crops such as groundnut, and
is addressed using Ammonium Sulphate or SSP, both of which supply
sulphur alongside nitrogen or phosphorus. Micronutrients such as zinc,
boron and iron are needed only in small quantities but their absence can
still sharply reduce yield: zinc deficiency causes small, narrow leaves
in maize and rice, boron deficiency causes poor flower and pod set in
groundnut, and iron deficiency causes yellowing between the veins of
young leaves, especially in soils with high pH.

SECTION 5: CROP-SPECIFIC FERTILIZER SCHEDULES
Rice typically receives a split application of Urea, with roughly half
applied at transplanting and the remainder split between tillering and
panicle initiation, plus a basal dose of DAP for early root growth.
Maize responds well to a basal dose of DAP and MOP followed by top
dressed Urea at the knee-high and tasseling stages, since it has a high
nitrogen demand during rapid vegetative growth. Wheat is usually given a
basal dose of DAP at sowing followed by Urea top dressing at the first
and second irrigation, timed to support tillering and grain filling.
Cotton benefits from a basal application of SSP for phosphorus and
sulphur together, with Urea applied in split doses through the
vegetative and boll development stages. Groundnut, being a legume, needs
comparatively little nitrogen but benefits from Ammonium Sulphate or SSP
at sowing to supply phosphorus and sulphur for pod development, along
with gypsum applied near flowering to support pod filling.

SECTION 6: COMMON DISEASES AND THEIR SYMPTOMS
Leaf Blight appears as yellow spots on leaves that gradually enlarge and
merge, weakening the plant's photosynthesis, and is treated with a
copper based fungicide. Powdery Mildew presents as a white powder
coating on leaves and stems, thriving in humid conditions with poor air
circulation, and is treated with a sulfur fungicide. Root Rot causes
root decay and wilting because damaged roots cannot take up water, and
the most effective response is improving field drainage rather than
spraying, since the underlying problem is excess soil moisture. Aphids
are small sap sucking insects that cause curling leaves and stunted
shoots, and are controlled using neem oil, which disrupts their feeding
and reproduction. Bacterial Wilt causes sudden wilting of the whole
plant even when soil moisture is adequate, because bacteria block the
plant's water conducting tissue, and infected plants should be removed
promptly to prevent the disease from spreading to neighboring plants.
Leaf Spot produces brown patches scattered across the leaf surface,
reducing the leaf area available for photosynthesis and lowering overall
yield if left untreated.

SECTION 7: PESTS AND INTEGRATED PEST MANAGEMENT
Aphids cluster on new shoots and the undersides of leaves, secreting
honeydew that attracts sooty mold; neem oil sprays and encouraging
natural predators such as ladybirds are commonly recommended controls.
The stem borer tunnels into maize and rice stalks during the larval
stage, causing dead hearts in young plants and empty panicles in
mature rice, and is managed through resistant varieties and timely
removal of stubble that harbors overwintering larvae. Whitefly feeds on
cotton and vegetable crops, transmitting viral diseases while also
weakening plants directly through sap removal, and is managed with
yellow sticky traps alongside targeted insecticide use when populations
exceed threshold levels. Nematodes are microscopic soil dwelling worms
that damage root systems, reducing water and nutrient uptake in crops
such as groundnut, and are managed through crop rotation with non host
crops and, where available, resistant varieties. Integrated pest
management combines these cultural, biological and chemical approaches
so that no single tactic is relied upon exclusively, which also slows
the development of pest resistance to any one treatment.

SECTION 8: TREATMENTS AND FIELD PRACTICES
Copper based fungicides act by disrupting fungal cell function on
contact and are most effective when applied at the first sign of leaf
spotting rather than after a disease has already spread widely. Sulfur
fungicides work well against powdery mildew but should be avoided during
very hot weather since sulfur can scorch leaves at high temperatures.
Improving drainage, whether through raised beds or field channels,
remains the most reliable long term treatment for root rot because it
addresses the excess moisture that allows the causal fungi to thrive.
Removing and destroying infected plant material promptly limits the
spread of bacterial wilt and other systemic diseases, since leaving
infected debris in the field allows the pathogen to persist between
seasons. Crop rotation, alternating cereal crops with legumes such as
groundnut, breaks the life cycle of many soil borne pests and diseases
while also improving soil nitrogen through the legume's nitrogen fixing
root nodules.

SECTION 9: WATER MANAGEMENT AND CLIMATE
Water stress during flowering and grain filling has a disproportionately
large effect on final yield compared with stress at other growth
stages, which is why irrigation scheduling often prioritizes these
windows even when overall water is limited. Waterlogging, the opposite
extreme, deprives roots of oxygen and encourages root rot, so fields
prone to standing water benefit from drainage channels even in crops
like rice that tolerate flooding during specific growth stages. Higher
than normal temperatures during flowering can reduce pollen viability in
crops such as maize and rice, leading to poor grain set even when water
and nutrients are otherwise adequate. Mulching the soil surface with
crop residue reduces evaporation, moderates soil temperature, and
suppresses weed growth, which is particularly useful for water sensitive
crops grown in sandy soil.

SECTION 10: HARVEST AND POST HARVEST NOTES
Rice is typically harvested when the grains have hardened and the straw
has turned golden yellow, since delayed harvest increases losses from
shattering and bird damage. Wheat is ready for harvest when the grain
is hard and the straw is dry, usually confirmed by biting a sample grain
to check that it no longer feels doughy. Maize cobs are left to dry in
the field until the husks turn brown before harvest, which reduces
storage moisture and lowers the risk of fungal growth in stored grain.
Cotton is picked in multiple rounds as bolls open over several weeks
rather than all at once, since bolls on the same plant mature at
different times. Proper drying and storage after harvest, keeping grain
moisture low and storage areas dry and pest free, prevents fungal
spoilage and preserves seed viability for the following season.
